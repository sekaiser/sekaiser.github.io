The design decisions behind djob

Published
15 Nov 2026
distributed-systems go system-design
On this page

A device job can outlive the process running it, the connection carrying its commands and the software release that created it. Designing djob has meant deciding which parts of that job should survive, who may act on its behalf, and when continuing would require evidence we no longer have.

My previous article compared several approaches to durable jobs. Here I want to explain the decisions behind my own: why controllers retain authority, why company code runs in independent workers, and why a lost worker can deliberately leave a bus unavailable. A small sizing example helps show which problems more workers can solve.

djob remains a public preview. The discussion follows inspected implementation contracts and uses a hypothetical device workload; it makes no claim about measured capacity or production reliability.

A meter reading is a stateful exchange

Consider meters behind a shared half-duplex bus: only one conversation can use it at a time. A job selects a meter, requests a reading, waits for the response and finishes the exchange. Another meter on the same bus may have a different address, but its job still competes for the same communication path.

For this article, assume one controller cluster managing a fleet of independent buses. Workers and network connections can fail. Devices may continue processing after a worker loses contact, and a command may have reached a gateway even when no response returns. Assume worker code obeys its execution and cleanup contracts. This is a failure model for the explanation, not a description of a qualified installation.

A run is one accepted execution of a job. An attempt is one worker's turn executing that run. An action is an individually authorized external operation within it. A resource identifies the thing competing jobs must not use together. I will call the example's run R and its bus B; these are explanatory labels, not API identifiers.

Suppose run R has selected its meter and is waiting for a reading when its worker disappears. A replacement process does not inherit that selection or establish whether the device answered. The controller needs to retain the recorded progress while keeping the unresolved device outcome and access to bus B explicit.

Estimating capacity from bus occupancy

A shared bus puts a ceiling on the work behind it. Suppose we have 100 independent buses, each needing 20 readings per minute. Assume a complete conversation occupies its bus for two seconds, including reply waits and cleanup. These numbers are a sizing exercise, not measurements of djob or a particular gateway.

A pool of workers can serve independent buses concurrently. It cannot make one serial bus perform two conversations safely at once. Nor does average utilization describe a burst: starting every periodic job on the minute can create a long wait even when the average rate fits.

Those independent buses still need enough workers. Each remote worker session serves one conversation at a time, including waits that retain the bus. Sustaining the offered rate therefore requires about 67 occupied conversations on average, with additional capacity for controller work and variation. Worker sessions, controller session limits and tenant quotas must all allow it. The inspected defaults permit four sessions per tenant per controller. Evenly spread over three controllers, that gives one tenant only 12 slots: insufficient for this workload. Adding worker Pods would reach the session ceiling; the example needs explicitly configured capacity. Worker sessions and capacity limits.

Little's law relates the average number of jobs in a system to its throughput and average time in that system: L = λW. Under stable operation with the required finite averages, a per-bus throughput of one reading every three seconds and a mean residence time of six seconds would imply two jobs in that system on average. Residence includes queueing and execution; the result says nothing about a tail-latency deadline.fn:1

This gives me quantities to measure rather than a capacity claim. I would check occupancy and queue age per bus, submission bursts, and time spent blocked after uncertain access. Each reading also causes several durable transitions. The fleet's roughly 33 readings per second are therefore not 33 storage writes per second. Record sizes, action count, retained results and commit latency need their own workload model.

Storage also has a lifetime cost. Request identities and run tombstones remain after eligible history is removed, so an old request cannot silently become new work after cleanup. Some receipt classes retain their full results permanently. At the example's rate, distinct readings would create 2.88 million runs a day even if every job finished promptly. Payload retention and compaction reclaim eligible data, but permanent metadata still needs a budget. Eventually that budget requires more storage or explicit deployment retirement. Expiring retry identities would be a different contract. Receipt compaction, Permanent run identities.

If arrivals continually exceed a bus's service capacity, keeping every accepted job merely preserves a growing backlog. Admission limits or a domain policy for rejecting obsolete readings become necessary. Coalescing may suit replaceable observations; it would be a different contract for a job whose every requested effect matters.

Independent releases for business code

A change to a company's meter protocol should be deployable without rebuilding the controllers. In the independent-worker model, the new implementation goes into a worker image that registers exact handler and driver versions. The controller selects compatible workers; it does not load the company's Go packages. That separation holds within the supported SDK and worker-protocol contracts. SDK dependencies and deployment choices.

Parnas's modular-design argument is useful here: hide design decisions likely to change behind interfaces, so changing them need not change the rest of the system.fn:2 The SDK exposes recorded state and execution requests as data, so company packages can depend on it without importing Raft or bbolt. The boundary separates protocol and business-code changes from the controller's persistence implementation.

A handler runs in the worker, receives recorded state, an event when present, and explicit time, then returns a bounded decision. The replicas apply the controller's validated state transition; they do not re-execute company handlers as part of log replay. A handler may itself run again after a failure, so its contract requires purity. External operations belong in the driver, which may retain a temporary session across related actions. The runtime relies on authors to follow these contracts; Go's type system does not enforce purity or bounded execution. Handler and driver contracts.

Independent deployment introduces a remote protocol. The SDK connects over authenticated gRPC/TLS and must handle command ordering, lost connections and cleanup. A worker process isolates its dependencies and ordinary crashes from controller processes, but it does not sandbox hostile code or verify that driver reports are truthful. Embedding company logic with a controller remains an option when deployment simplicity outweighs that separation.

Job claims and reservations commit together

A job claim and its resource reservations must agree: claiming run R while leaving bus B available would admit conflicting work. The controllers use Raft to order transitions and apply them deterministically to replicated state. Raft supplies agreement on that order; the application transaction defines which changes succeed or fail together.fn:3

The claim must check compatibility, quotas, grants and every required resource before assigning ownership. djob applies these changes through one transactional state transition. If bus B is busy, the rejected claim leaves no partial reservation for the run to hold while it waits for something else. Claim and ownership transitions.

Putting resource ownership in a separate lock service is a credible alternative. It also introduces a boundary between accepting a job claim and acquiring its resources. An application has to reconcile that boundary after failures. Keeping them together makes this invariant easier to express, at the cost of routing coordination through the same replicated state machine.

Each controller member retains a WAL, snapshots and materialized bbolt state. Persistent ready and wait indexes are transactional access paths over that state; some derived views have explicit rebuild procedures. I would not infer from the diagram that every index is a disposable cache or that restarting a member reconstructs the outcome of a missing external response. Raft persistence and application, Dispatch indexes.

For the worked example, a three-controller cluster needs two members for a majority. Losing that majority prevents new committed authority. Work already authorized still has to obey its bounded permission and cleanup rules; a quorum outage does not retract a command from a device. Controller checks of quorum and locally applied state help govern remote sessions, while device-side exclusion remains a separate obligation. Remote-worker quorum checks.

The claim, each action authorization and each recorded result add work to the persistence and replication path. These commits contribute latency even when the bus is idle. The capacity model therefore needs both conversation occupancy and the number and cost of durable transitions per conversation.

When a device acts but the result is lost

Suppose the selected worker asks to read a meter. Its handler's decision grants no I/O permission. The controller first records an authorized action with a run identity, attempt identity and action sequence. The driver can then perform that operation while its permission remains valid. The controller must commit the reported effect before advancing from it.

This ordering keeps recoverable coordination facts around an operation whose physical outcome may be unrecoverable. It deliberately leaves a gap: after authorization, the worker might fail before sending, while sending, or after the device acts. If the result never commits, the log cannot tell those cases apart.

Birrell and Nelson's RPC analysis describes this ambiguity after a failed call: the caller may be unable to establish whether the procedure executed.fn:4 Recording more controller state does not remove that uncertainty. A receiver that atomically deduplicates a stable operation identity can make repetition safe for that operation; a legacy device may offer no equivalent contract.

The end-to-end argument helps place the acknowledgement we actually need. Saltzer, Reed and Clark distinguish lower-level delivery from completion of an application's requirement.fn:5 An MQTT broker accepting a command establishes a transport fact. A device reply establishes only what its protocol promises. A worker report received by a controller still needs the engine's durable commit. Those acknowledgements serve different purposes.

Suppose meter selection has a committed success result, but the following read has only an authorization record. That history tells the operator which step lacks an outcome; it does not establish whether a reading returned or whether the bus is safe. Retaining those intermediate facts can help reconciliation. It also costs the commits described above. If nothing inside the exchange has an independently useful result, treating the whole conversation as one action can be a reasonable boundary. Finer actions earn their cost when their recorded outcomes help the application or operator make a decision.

A replacement worker does not receive permission to replay an uncertain action simply because it can reconnect. Run R keeps its recorded history, but the lost device session cannot be reconstructed from it. When the execution rules permit another attempt, that attempt starts with a fresh driver. Session supervision, Action completion and expiry.

Resource reservations across waits and failures

Bus B has to name the physical contention boundary. Giving each meter its own resource would allow two correctly claimed jobs to collide on the same wire. The inventory must map every relevant alias, address and tenant to the shared resource, and the integration must account for access that bypasses the scheduler.

The reservation covers commands, reply waits and cleanup. A resource-free wait can end an attempt and later resume elsewhere. A wait that retains the bus keeps its conversation and reservation together. Releasing the bus during that wait would improve apparent utilization by allowing an exchange that may interfere with the first one.

Leases and ownership epochs help reject stale controller-side operations. Physical enforcement needs more. Chubby's sequencers illustrate the necessary cooperation: the protected recipient checks the generation and rejects an obsolete operation.fn:6 If a gateway cannot do that, a replacement controller cannot turn its newer epoch into a physical fence.

The driver therefore has specific obligations. Execute returns only after its I/O stops. Quiesce includes recovery and protocol quiet periods; Close releases local sessions. Even a closed connection can leave a queued command or an active device. The SDK does not detach an uncooperative driver and pretend its session is safe.

When access remains uncertain, djob blocks the resource. That protects its admission rule while sacrificing availability for every job sharing the bus. A fixed timeout or another Pod does not establish that the previous access stopped. An integration or operator needs evidence that excludes it.

Operators can resolve outcome and access separately. After establishing that the old access stopped, an operator can release B for other work while R remains unknown and undispatched. Resolving R means settling it as succeeded, failed or cancelled; it does not resume the interrupted conversation. That differs from the normal resource-free wait, which can resume later. Conversely, evidence of the job's success may settle R while B stays blocked. A checkpoint stores logical progress; it cannot restore a selected device, a socket or an unfinished handshake.

Release pinning and configuration identity

Run R may still be waiting for a device when release v2 is deployed. If the new handler interprets its checkpoint differently, moving the run to that code would change its meaning even though the data survived. Compatibility with the worker protocol alone does not establish compatibility with an unfinished job's state.

djob can select a published worker release when it accepts a run. That selection stays with the run through retries and resumptions. Old and new releases can coexist. Weighted routing selects a release for new work; it is a deterministic allocation from run identities, not a promise of an exact percentage in a small sample or a load balancer. Existing trigger templates retain their own snapshots until explicitly updated. Release admission and outstanding work, Run activation and release selection.

This moves a compatibility obligation into deployment. Operators retain the selected worker implementation until its accepted work finishes, including waits and unknown outcomes. Retirement can stop new admission without migrating old runs. Managed lifecycle guards cannot prevent an external Kubernetes eviction or infrastructure failure.

The order matters when changing a weighted policy. Suppose a trigger retains a snapshot assigning 20% to v1 and 80% to v2. Closing v1 admission blocks all new runs through that policy, including identities that would select v2: every listed release must be active before selection. There is no automatic redistribution. Update the definition and affected trigger snapshots to remove v1 first, then close its admission while retaining it for accepted work. Updating only the definition leaves existing trigger snapshots unchanged. Weighted-policy validation.

An image digest is also only part of execution identity. A configuration reference can keep its name while its contents change. The optional immutable-configuration policy checks protected Kubernetes Secrets and pins their UID; it needs the corresponding admission policy and permissions. A default reference alone does not freeze those bytes. Kubernetes configuration checks.

Release pinning spends retained deployment capacity to keep old execution semantics available. A system with a thoroughly specified state migration could choose differently; that migration would need its own handling of in-flight operations and protocol sessions.

Recovery requests must match the recorded state

An operator may inspect an unknown run while another operator is already resolving it. Recovery requests therefore need a precondition tied to the state that was inspected. Resolving a run checks its expected checkpoint step; releasing a resource checks its blocked state and ownership epoch, the generation number for that reservation. If that state has changed, the request fails instead of applying a decision to a different situation. Run and resource resolution.

Authentication and tenant checks restrict who may submit those decisions. The controller also records the actor and reason. These checks make recovery an attributable state transition; the operator must still supply evidence for the outcome or for stopped access. A recorded resolution cannot make a delayed device command disappear. Tenant-scoped APIs likewise cannot exclude an out-of-band client from the same physical bus.

The operational signals should distinguish the work needed next. A growing queue behind a busy bus calls for examining arrival rate and occupancy. A blocked bus calls for establishing safe access before releasing it. Unknown runs that prevent release retirement need reconciliation, even when spare workers are available. Treating all three as a shortage of workers would lead to the wrong intervention.

Cluster recovery has a similar authority boundary. Replacing one member while a quorum survives preserves the existing cluster's authority. A temporary quorum outage with intact durable state differs from losing the storage needed to recover that history. Restoring from an older backup must exclude the old cluster, but it also has a recovery point: operations accepted after the backup's boundary may be absent.

Imagine that the backup shows B available, then the original cluster accepts a reading and the device acts. If that later history is lost, the restored database may contain neither the request identity nor an unknown run that reveals the missing operation. Fencing the old workers cannot recover those facts. Recovered execution starts held; producers must remain paused while operators reconcile restored work and external effects. An absent record is insufficient evidence for resubmission. Backup recovery and execution hold.

The worker fault cases and release-admission cases cover parts of the execution contract; a target installation still needs its own storage and physical-exclusion qualification.

Why I chose to build the engine

I wanted a balance between components I could keep understandable, the device jobs I needed to support, and a direct path through the storage and execution logic. Performance mattered, and so did learning these technologies by building an application with real constraints. CockroachDB's architecture was an inspiration for my work on consensus. Adopting a distributed SQL database would have solved a broader problem than this engine needs.

The controller accesses job state through known keys and indexes. bbolt provides embedded key/value transactions that fit those access patterns; the application has no requirement for SQL queries. I use etcd Raft to order replicated changes and bbolt to apply local state transactions. Keeping storage inside each controller avoids a separate database service. I consider that a sufficient foundation for this design, while its capacity still depends on the workload and storage hardware.

A SQL-backed implementation would also be credible. It could keep claims, reservations and results together in short transactions, with a gateway owner handling the live conversation outside them. PostgreSQL's row locks, for example, provide a way to coordinate concurrent changes. Such a design would still need the device-specific uncertainty and cleanup rules. If I already operated a suitable database, that could be a practical starting point. My choice trades that dependency for responsibility for replication, snapshots and recovery inside the project.

Temporal is another credible choice. It supplies durable workflow history, activities, timers, independent workers and Worker Versioning, including pinned workflows. In the meter example, a gateway owner could keep the bus conversation inside one activity, while a resource coordinator governed access across jobs. That adds an application boundary to reconcile, but carries much of the surrounding workflow machinery. I would weigh it seriously for a process already using Temporal. Worker Versioning, worker deployments.

Building djob lets me develop the run, resource reservation, uncertain outcome and selected release as one execution model. The compact storage component helps me understand that model; it does not remove the complexity of a distributed system. The project owns consensus integration, storage behavior, protocol compatibility and operational qualification. I have no shared experiment establishing a throughput, reliability or cost advantage over a SQL-backed design or Temporal.

An adopter also has to consider the license: the engine is source-available under custom terms, while the Go SDK is Apache 2.0. Engine and SDK licensing.

What would change the design

The sizing example gives me a place to start: measure service time and queue age at the bus, check worker-session capacity, then account for controller work and lifetime storage growth. If the replicated commit path becomes the measured bottleneck while independent buses have capacity, partitioning independent resource domains could become useful. Splitting work across clusters would also require a clear ownership boundary; a resource shared across them would reintroduce coordination.

If gateways gain enforceable fencing and operation deduplication, recovery could become more available without guessing what an old worker did. If the workload becomes mostly resource-free branching workflows, the case for a specialized conversation engine weakens. If uncertain runs prevent release retirement for too long, the missing capability may be a better reconciliation process rather than more worker replicas.
