Keeping current data useful

Published
02 Nov 2026
backpressure system-design telemetry
On this page

A dashboard can respond quickly and still leave someone unsure whether a reading is current. The design has to account for what has arrived, what is still waiting, and what may have been lost.

These three articles approach that problem from different directions. Start with the cache design if you are deciding how to serve current readings. If your immediate problem is overload or a delayed read, go straight to that section below.

Start with the view someone needs

From Time-Series to State starts with a slow table of device readings. It follows the change from reconstructing current state on every request to maintaining the latest observation for each device and metric.

Read it for the relationship between the stored fields and the work each query has to do. The encoding diagram explains how individual metrics can be updated and read without rewriting an entire device. The freshness discussion keeps the age of an observation separate from the age of an assembled response.

Before choosing a snapshot lifetime, consider what someone uses the view to decide. A recently assembled response can contain an old observation from a device that has gone quiet. Its timestamps still need to make that visible.

Decide what can be lost during overload

Maintaining that view creates work on the ingestion path. Backpressure in a Telemetry Pipeline examines a bounded cache-update branch that can replace or discard pending work while required publication continues.

Read it when a queue keeps growing, or when adding workers has stopped helping. The low-level diagram follows admission, claiming and writing. The trade-off sections explain why a memory bound does not establish a freshness guarantee.

The awkward case is a device's final reading. If that update is dropped and the device stops reporting, later readings from other devices cannot repair the gap. Whether that is acceptable belongs in the view's requirements, along with how people can recognize degraded data and how repair will work.

Know when a write becomes visible

Fast Writes, Fresh Reads uses a separate journal experiment in Go and Rust to examine the gap between recording an update and making it available through a read-optimized view.

Start here when a write succeeds but the screen still shows an earlier value. The article compares scanning on demand, updating a projection before replying, and letting a background worker maintain it. The freshness position gives a reader a way to tell how far the view has progressed; the lag limit puts a budget on unfinished work.

The local measurements compare those policies under stated conditions. They do not establish the capacity of a deployed telemetry system. Also note what each design means by latest: the cache article compares observation timestamps, while the journal experiment applies records in journal order. That choice changes how a delayed observation affects the result.

Bring the questions back to your system

For a concrete feature, describe what someone needs to know from the view and what they can tolerate while it catches up. Then follow one update from acceptance to visibility. Identify the places it can wait, be superseded or be discarded, and who would notice if it never arrived.

Those answers help decide whether to reuse a snapshot, slow admission, or provide repair. They also give the people building and operating the system something specific to discuss before changing a queue size or adding another worker.
